**A ratio that is near-constant across conditions is the most flattering possible way to be
wrong.** It reads as "one clean level offset, one cause" — which is exactly the shape that makes
a beat name an inherited gap and stop. Before accepting it, divide it back out and read each half
against the source on its own.

D-255 §5 (corrected at D-256). The model's isoamyl-alcohol enrichment on Rollero's three musts
came out at 0.56× / 0.66× / 0.58× of his measurement, and the record called it "a uniform level
shortfall, the inherited D-111 gap". Both halves say otherwise. The **labelled** amount runs
0.56× / 1.03× / 1.73× (valine tracer) and 0.29× / 0.71× / 1.07× (leucine), crossing from under to
over; the **total** runs 1.00× / 1.55× / 3.00×. Two quantities over-responding to must nitrogen at
similar rates, whose quotient happens to sit near a constant. There was no level offset to close.

**Why:** an enrichment, a share, a yield, an efficiency — every ratio observable hides its own
denominator, and the denominator is usually the quantity the model is *worse* at, because it is
the bulk one. Constancy across conditions feels like evidence of a single cause; it is equally
the signature of two causes with the same driver. Here the driver was nitrogen, and it moved both
halves, so the ratio told you nothing about either.

**How to apply:** when a source prints a ratio, transcribe the numerator and the denominator too,
not just the percentage, and score all three. If the source only prints the ratio, say so and
mark the finding as un-attributable rather than assigning it a cause. And treat a flat
model/measured ratio as a prompt to look for a shared driver, not as a licence to call it a
calibration offset. **This is the second consecutive beat where a ratio read as a level claim was
a frame artefact** — D-251 was the first, where two fifths of a residue was the frame.
